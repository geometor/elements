Book Two, Proposition Twelve. In obtuse-angled triangles, the square on the side subtending the obtuse angle is greater than the squares on the sides containing the obtuse angle by twice the rectangle contained by one of the sides about the obtuse angle, namely that on which the perpendicular falls, and the segment cut off outside by the perpendicular towards the obtuse angle. <pause> Let A B C be an obtuse-angled triangle having the angle B A C obtuse.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Produce C A in a straight line, and from B draw B D perpendicular to C A produced. <pause> Since the segment C D has been cut at random at A, by Two Four the square on C D equals the squares on C A and A D together with twice the rectangle contained by C A and A D.

Add the square on D B to each. Then the squares on C D and D B equal the squares on C A, A D, and D B together with twice the rectangle C A, A D. <pause> Because the angle at D is right, by One Forty-Seven the square on C B equals the squares on C D and D B, and the square on A B equals the squares on A D and D B. <pause> Therefore, the square on B C equals the squares on B A and A C together with twice the rectangle contained by C A and A D—and so exceeds the squares on B A and A C by twice that rectangle.
