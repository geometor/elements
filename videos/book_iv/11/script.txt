Book Four, Proposition Eleven. In a given circle to inscribe an equilateral and equiangular pentagon. <pause> Let A B C D E be the given circle; it is required to inscribe in the circle A B C D E an equilateral and equiangular pentagon.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
By Four Ten, set out the golden isosceles triangle F G H having each of its base angles at G and H double the vertex angle at F. By Four Two, inscribe in circle A B C D E the triangle A C D equiangular with triangle F G H, so that each of the base angles A C D and C D A is double the angle C A D. <pause> By One Nine, bisect angles A C D and C D A respectively by the chords C E and D B, and join A B, B C, D E, and E A.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because each of the angles A C D and C D A is double angle C A D and has been bisected by C E and D B, the five inscribed angles D A C, A C E, E C D, C D B, and B D A are all equal to one another. By Three Twenty-Six, equal inscribed angles stand on equal arcs, so the five arcs A B, B C, C D, D E, and E A are equal; and by Three Twenty-Nine, equal arcs are subtended by equal chords, so sides A B, B C, C D, D E, and E A are equal. Therefore the pentagon A B C D E is equilateral. <pause> Next, because arc A B equals arc D E, add arc B C D to each: the whole arc A B C D equals the whole arc E D C B. By Three Twenty-Seven, the inscribed angles A E D and B A E standing on those arcs are equal, and by the same reasoning all five interior angles B A E, A B C, B C D, C D E, and A E D are equal to one another. <pause> Therefore an equilateral and equiangular pentagon A B C D E has been inscribed in the given circle, as was required to do.

[bell:system]
[style: reflective, scholarly, architectural synthesis]
Proposition Four Eleven crowns the Pythagorean theory of regular polygons in the circle. As Heath remarks against De Morgan, rather than stepping off a decagon around the circumference, Euclid inscribes the golden triangle A C D and bisects its base angles with chords C E and D B—tracing four of the five lines of the Pythagorean pentagram-star right inside the circle. Each of the five thirty-six-degree inscribed angles intercepts one-fifth of the circumference, while each interior pentagon angle spans three-fifths of the circle, or one hundred eight degrees. <pause> In the GEOMETOR G-Index, node G one hundred sixty-three unites twelve direct rules across one hundred eight ancestor nodes, directly enabling the circumscribed pentagon in Four Twelve, the fifteen-gon in Four Sixteen, and the Platonic solids of Book Thirteen.
