Book Four, Proposition Sixteen. In a given circle to inscribe a fifteen-angled figure which shall be both equilateral and equiangular. <pause> Let A B C D be the given circle; it is required to inscribe in the circle A B C D a fifteen-angled figure which shall be both equilateral and equiangular.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
In the circle A B C D, from vertex A, inscribe a side A C of the equilateral triangle inscribed in it by Four Two, and a side A B of an equilateral pentagon by Four Eleven. <pause> Then bisect the arc B C at E by Three Thirty, and join the chords B E and E C.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Suppose the circumference of the circle A B C D divided into fifteen equal parts. Because the equilateral triangle divides the circle into thirds, the arc A B C contains five of those fifteen parts; and because the regular pentagon divides the circle into fifths, the arc A B contains three of those fifteen parts. Therefore the remainder, arc B C, contains two of the equal parts. <pause> Since arc B C is bisected at E, each of the arcs B E and E C is one-fifteenth of the whole circle. If therefore we join B E and E C and fit straight lines equal to them continuously around the circle A B C D, a fifteen-angled figure which is both equilateral and equiangular has been inscribed in it. Which was to be done.

[bell:system]
[style: reflective, scholarly, architectural synthesis]
Proposition Four Sixteen crowns Book Four by combining the equilateral triangle and the golden-ratio pentagon through the harmonic identity one-third minus one-fifth equals two-fifteenths. As Proclus records, ancient astronomers prized the fifteen-angled figure because its central angle of twenty-four degrees measured the obliquity of the ecliptic—the tilt between the celestial equator and the zodiac. <pause> Together with continual arc bisection, Euclid's constructions of the three-gon, four-gon, five-gon, and fifteen-gon exhausted all known constructible regular polygons for over two millennia, until Carl Friedrich Gauss proved in seventeen ninety-six that a regular n-gon is constructible by straightedge and compass if and only if its odd prime factors are distinct Fermat primes—beginning with three and five, whose product is fifteen.
