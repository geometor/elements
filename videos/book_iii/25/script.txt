Book Three, Proposition Twenty-Five. Given a segment of a circle, to describe the complete circle of which it is a segment. <pause> Let A B C be the given segment of a circle. It is required to construct the complete circle of which A B C is a segment.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Bisect the chord A C at D by One Ten, draw D B from D at right angles to A C by One Eleven, and join A B by Postulate One. <pause> Angle A B D is then either greater than, equal to, or less than angle B A D. First let it be greater. On B A at point A, construct angle B A E equal to angle A B D by One Twenty-Three; extend D B to meet A E at E by Postulate Two, and join E C by Postulate One.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because angle A B E equals angle B A E, in triangle A B E the sides E B and E A are equal by One Six. <pause> Next compare triangles A D E and C D E: A D equals D C, D E is common, and the right angles A D E and C D E are equal; so by One Four the bases A E and C E are equal. <pause> Since A E equals both E B and E C, the three segments E A, E B, and E C are all equal. By Three Nine, point E—from which three equal segments fall on the arc—is the center of the circle; and constructing circle E A, with center E and defining radius point A, completes the circle whether the segment is less than, equal to, or greater than a semicircle, as was required to do.
