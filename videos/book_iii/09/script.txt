Book Three, Proposition Nine. If a point be taken within a circle, and more than two equal segments fall from the point on the circle, the point taken is the center of the circle. <pause> Let A B C be a circle and D a point within it, and from D let more than two equal segments, namely D A, D B, and D C, fall on the circle A B C. I say that the point D is the center of the circle.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Join the chords A B and B C by Postulate One, and bisect them at the points E and F by One Ten. Then join E D and F D, and extend them across the circle to the points G, K, H, and L.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because A E equals E B, E D is common, and base D A equals base D B, the triangles A E D and B E D are congruent by One Eight. Therefore angle A E D equals angle B E D, so each is a right angle by Definition Ten, and G K bisects the chord A B at right angles. <pause> By the Porism to Three One, the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the center of the circle; hence the center of A B C lies on G K. By the exact same reasoning on chord B C, the center also lies on H L. <pause> Since the two straight lines G K and H L have no point in common except D, the point D is the center of the circle A B C. As was required to prove.

[bell:system]
[style: reflective, scholarly, architectural synthesis]
Already cited by Aristotle in the Meteorologica, Proposition Three Nine is the logical contrapositive of Three Seven: since no eccentric point can send three equal segments to a circle, any point that does must be the center. <pause> Rather than relying on indirect proof from Three Seven as Simson later did, Heiberg's critical edition preserves Euclid's direct constructive proof—intersecting two perpendicular chord bisectors from Three One across thirty-four ancestor nodes in the G-Index.
