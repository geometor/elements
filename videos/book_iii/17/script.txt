Book Three, Proposition Seventeen. From a given point, a straight line is drawn touching a given circle. <pause> Let A be the given external point, and let B C D be the given circle; we seek the straight line from the point A that touches the circle B C D.

[bell:construction]
[style:brisk]
By Three One, we take the center E of the circle B C D, join A E cutting the circle at D, and with center E and radius E A describe the concentric circle A F G. <pause> From D, we raise D F at right angles to E A meeting circle A F G at F, join E F cutting circle B C D at B, and join A B.

[bell:insight]
[style:brisk]
Because E is the common center of circles B C D and A F G, the outer radii E A and E F are equal, and the inner radii E D and E B are equal. Since sides A E and E B equal sides F E and E D and contain the common angle at E, by One Four triangles D E F and B E A are congruent, so angle E B A equals angle E D F. But angle E D F was constructed right; therefore angle E B A is also a right angle. <pause> Now E B is a radius, and by the Porism of Three Sixteen, the straight line at right angles to the diameter of a circle from its extremity touches the circle. Therefore A B has been drawn from the given point A touching the circle B C D, as was required to do.

[bell:system]
[style:resonant]
Euclid's concentric-circle construction in Three Seventeen ingeniously avoids using the inscribed right angle in a semicircle, which is not proved until Three Thirty-One. As Heron and Heath note, extending D F to both sides of E A immediately yields two equal tangents from A equally inclined to the line of centers. <pause> In the GEOMETOR G-Index, node G one hundred nineteen combines circle center finding from Three One, SAS congruence from One Four, and the tangent criterion of Three Sixteen across forty-six ancestor nodes.
