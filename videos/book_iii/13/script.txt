Book Three, Proposition Thirteen. A circle does not touch a circle at more points than one, whether it touch it internally or externally. <pause> For, if possible, let the circle A B D C touch the circle E B F D, first internally, at more points than one, namely B and D.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Let the center G of circle A B D C, and the center H of circle E B F D, be taken. By Three Eleven, the straight line joined from G to H and produced falls on the points of internal contact B and D, as B G H D.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because G is the center of circle A B D C, the radius B G equals G D. Since G D contains H D, B G is greater than H D, and therefore the whole segment B H is much greater than H D. Yet H is the center of circle E B F D, so B H also equals H D—which is impossible. Thus two circles cannot touch internally at more than one point. <pause> Nor can they touch externally at two points. For if circle A C K touches circle A B D C externally at A and C, join the chord A C. By Three Two, the chord joining two points on a circle falls within each circle; yet because the circles touch externally, A C falls outside circle A C K—which is absurd. <pause> Therefore, a circle does not touch a circle at more points than one, whether internally or externally.
