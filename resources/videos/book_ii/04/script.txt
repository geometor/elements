Book Two, Proposition Four. If a segment be cut at random, the square on the whole is equal to the squares on the segments and twice the rectangle contained by the segments. <pause> Let the segment A B be cut at random at C.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
On A B, describe the square A D E B by One Forty-Six, and join the diagonal B D. <pause> Through C, draw C F parallel to A D or B E by One Thirty-One, meeting B D at G; and through G, draw H K parallel to A B or D E.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because C F is parallel to A D, the exterior angle C G B equals the interior angle A D B by One Twenty-Nine. And since A B equals A D, angle A D B equals angle A B D by One Five. Therefore angle C G B equals angle G B C, so side B C equals C G by One Six, and by One Thirty-Four all four sides of C G K B are equal. <pause> Moreover, since C G is parallel to B K and angle K B C is right, all four angles of C G K B are right angles. Thus C G K B is the square on B C, and for the same reason H F is the square on H G, which equals A C. <pause> Now the complements A G and G E are equal, and since C G equals B C, each is the rectangle contained by A C and B C, so together they equal twice that rectangle. <pause> Adding the two squares H F and C K, the four areas compose the whole square A D E B. Therefore, the square on A B equals the squares on A C and B C plus twice the rectangle contained by A C and B C. Which was to be proved.
