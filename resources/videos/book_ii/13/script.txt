Book Two, Proposition Thirteen. In acute-angled triangles, the square on the side subtending the acute angle is less than the squares on the sides containing the acute angle by twice the rectangle contained by one of the sides about the acute angle, namely that on which the perpendicular falls, and the segment cut off within by the perpendicular towards the acute angle. <pause> Let A B C be an acute-angled triangle having the angle at B acute.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
From A, draw A D perpendicular to B C. <pause> Since the segment C B has been cut at random at D, by Two Seven the squares on C B and B D are equal to twice the rectangle contained by C B and B D together with the square on D C.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Add the square on D A to each. Then the squares on C B, B D, and D A equal twice the rectangle C B, B D together with the squares on A D and D C. <pause> Because the angle at D is right, by One Forty-Seven the square on A B equals the squares on B D and D A, and the square on A C equals the squares on A D and D C. <pause> Therefore, the squares on C B and B A equal the square on A C together with twice the rectangle contained by C B and B D—so the square on A C alone is less than the squares on C B and B A by twice that rectangle. Which was to be proved.
