Book Four, Proposition Four. In a given triangle to inscribe a circle. <pause> Let A B C be the given triangle; it is required to inscribe a circle in the triangle A B C.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Bisect the angles A B C and A C B by the straight lines B D and C D by One Nine, and let them meet one another at the point D. <pause> From D, draw D E, D F, and D G perpendicular to the sides A B, B C, and C A.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because angle A B D equals angle C B D, the right angle B E D equals the right angle B F D, and the hypotenuse B D is common, by One Twenty-Six the triangles E B D and F B D have their remaining sides equal, so D E equals D F. For the same reason, D G also equals D F; therefore all three perpendiculars D E, D F, and D G are equal to one another. <pause> Hence the circle described with center D and distance D E passes through F and G, and touches A B, B C, and C A because the angles at E, F, and G are right—for if it cut them, a line drawn at right angles to a diameter from its extremity would fall within the circle, which was proved absurd in Three Sixteen. <pause> Let the circle E F G be described; therefore in the given triangle A B C the circle E F G has been inscribed. Which was to be done.
