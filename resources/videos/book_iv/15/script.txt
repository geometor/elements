Book Four, Proposition Fifteen. In a given circle to inscribe an equilateral and equiangular hexagon. <pause> Let A B C D E F be the given circle; it is required to inscribe an equilateral and equiangular hexagon in the circle A B C D E F.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Draw the diameter A D of the circle, take the center G, and with center D and radius D G describe the circle E G C H. <pause> Join E G and C G, carry them through to the points B and F, and join A B, B C, C D, D E, E F, and F A.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because G is the center of circle A B C D E F and D is the center of circle E G C H, the segments G E, G D, and D E are all equal. Thus triangle E G D—and similarly triangle D G C—is equilateral, so by One Five and One Thirty-Two the angles E G D and D G C are each one-third of two right angles. Since C G standing on E B makes adjacent angles equal to two right angles, the remaining angle C G B is also one-third of two right angles, and by One Fifteen the vertical angles B G A, A G F, and F G E are equal to them; hence all six central angles at G are equal. <pause> By Three Twenty-Six, equal central angles stand on equal arcs A B, B C, C D, D E, E F, and F A; and by Three Twenty-Nine, equal arcs are subtended by equal chords, so the hexagon A B C D E F is equilateral. <pause> Finally, adding arc A B C D to the equal arcs F A and D E makes arc F A B C D equal to arc E D C B A, so by Three Twenty-Seven angle F E D equals angle A F E, and all six interior angles are equal. Therefore the equilateral and equiangular hexagon A B C D E F has been inscribed in the circle, and its side equals the radius. Which was to be done.
