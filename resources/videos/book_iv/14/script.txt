Book Four, Proposition Fourteen. About a given pentagon, which is equilateral and equiangular, to circumscribe a circle. <pause> Let A B C D E be the given equilateral and equiangular pentagon; it is required to circumscribe a circle about the pentagon A B C D E.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Bisect the adjacent angles B C D and C D E by the straight lines F C and F D, and from the point F at which they meet, join the straight lines F B, F A, and F E to the vertices B, A, and E.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
In the same manner as in the preceding proposition, it is proved that the angles C B A, B A E, and A E D are also bisected by F B, F A, and F E. And because angle B C D equals angle C D E, their halves—angle F C D and angle C D F—are also equal. <pause> Therefore in triangle F C D, by One Six, the side F C equals the side F D; and by the same reasoning all five radial segments F A, F B, F C, F D, and F E are equal to one another. <pause> Therefore the circle described with center F and radius F A passes through all five vertices A, B, C, D, and E. Let it be circumscribed as circle A B C D E. Thus a circle has been circumscribed about the given equilateral and equiangular pentagon. Which was to be done.
