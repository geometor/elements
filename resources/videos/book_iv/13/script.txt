Book Four, Proposition Thirteen. In a given pentagon, which is equilateral and equiangular, to inscribe a circle. <pause> Let A B C D E be the given equilateral and equiangular pentagon; it is required to inscribe a circle in the pentagon A B C D E.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Bisect the adjacent angles B C D and C D E by the straight lines C F and D F, and from the point F where they meet, join F B, F A, and F E. <pause> From F, draw F G, F H, F K, F L, and F M perpendicular to the five sides A B, B C, C D, D E, and E A.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Because B C equals C D, C F is common, and angle B C F equals angle D C F, by One Four triangles B C F and D C F are congruent, so angle C B F equals angle C D F. Since angle A B C equals angle C D E, which is double angle C D F, angle A B C is also double angle C B F; thus F B—and similarly F A and F E—bisects its vertex angle. <pause> Now in right triangles F H C and F K C, the angles at C are equal, the right angles at H and K are equal, and the hypotenuse F C is common. By One Twenty-Six, F H equals F K, and by the same reasoning all five perpendiculars F G, F H, F K, F L, and F M are equal. <pause> Therefore the circle described with center F and radius F G passes through G, H, K, L, and M, and by Three Sixteen touches all five sides at right angles. Thus circle G H K L M has been inscribed in the pentagon A B C D E. Which was to be done.
