Book Four, Proposition Eight. In a given square to inscribe a circle. <pause> Let A B C D be the given square; it is required to inscribe a circle in the given square A B C D.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Bisect the sides A D and A B at the points E and F respectively by One Ten. <pause> Through E draw E H parallel to A B or C D, and through F draw F K parallel to A D or B C by One Thirty-One, intersecting at G.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Each of the resulting sub-figures is a parallelogram, so their opposite sides are equal by One Thirty-Four. Because A D equals A B, their halves A E and A F are equal, making the opposite sides F G and G E equal; and similarly G H and G K equal F G and G E, so all four segments G E, G F, G H, and G K are equal to one another. <pause> Therefore the circle described with center G and radius G E passes through F, H, and K; and because the angles at E, F, H, and K are right, by Three Sixteen the circle cannot cut the sides of the square, lest a line at right angles to a diameter at its extremity fall within the circle. <pause> Thus the circle touches all four sides A B, B C, C D, and D A, and has been inscribed in the given square A B C D. Which was to be done.

[bell:system]
[style: reflective, scholarly, architectural synthesis]
Proposition Four Eight completes Euclid's quartet of circle-and-square constructions by inscribing a circle in a given square. As Heath notes in his commentary, because a quadrilateral admits an inscribed circle whenever the sums of its opposite sides are equal, a circle can be inscribed in any square or rhombus, but never in an oblong rectangle or rhomboid. <pause> In the GEOMETOR G-Index, node G one hundred sixty unites segment bisection, parallel lines, and parallelogram symmetry with the tangent criterion of Three Sixteen across forty-nine ancestor nodes.
