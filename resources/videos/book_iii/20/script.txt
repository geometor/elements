Book Three, Proposition Twenty. In a circle, the angle at the center is double of the angle at the circumference, when the angles have the same circumference as base. <pause> Let A B C be a circle, let the angle B E C be an angle at its center E, and the angle B A C an angle at the circumference, having the same arc B C as base. We claim that angle B E C is double of angle B A C.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Join A E and draw it through to F. And for the second case, let another angle B D C be inflected at the circumference, and join D E and produce it to G.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
Since the radius E A equals the radius E B, by One Five the base angle E A B equals the base angle E B A; therefore the sum of angles E A B and E B A is double of angle E A B. But by One Thirty-Two, the exterior angle B E F of triangle E A B equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles E A B and E B A; therefore angle B E F is double of angle E A B. <pause> For the same reason, in triangle E A C, the exterior angle F E C is double of angle E A C. Therefore the whole central angle B E C is double of the whole inscribed angle B A C. <pause> Again, for the inflected angle B D C, angle G E C is similarly double of angle E D C, of which angle G E B is double of angle E D B; therefore the remaining angle B E C is double of the remaining angle B D C. As was required to prove.
