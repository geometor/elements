Book Three, Proposition Seven. If on the diameter of a circle a point be taken which is not the center of the circle, and from the point segments fall upon the circle, that will be greatest on which the center is, the remainder of the same diameter will be least, of the rest the nearer to the segment through the center is always greater than the more remote, and only two equal segments will fall from the point on the circle, one on each side of the least. <pause> Let A B C D be a circle with diameter A D and center E, let F be a point on A D other than E, and from F let F B, F C, and F G fall upon the circle.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Join the radii B E, C E, and G E by Postulate One. Then on E F at the center E, construct the angle F E H equal to angle G E F by One Twenty-Three, and join F H.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
In triangle B E F, the two sides E B and E F are greater than B F by One Twenty. Since radius A E equals E B, the whole segment F A equals E B plus E F, so F A is greater than F B. Moreover, in triangles B E F and C E F, radii B E and C E are equal, E F is common, and angle B E F is greater than angle C E F; by the Hinge Theorem, One Twenty-Four, F B is greater than F C, and by the same reason F C is greater than F G. <pause> Next, in triangle G E F, G F plus F E is greater than radius E G, which equals E D. Subtracting E F from each leaves F G greater than F D; thus F D is least. <pause> Because G E equals E H, E F is common, and angle G E F equals angle F E H, triangles G E F and H E F are congruent by One Four, so F G equals F H. If any third segment F K were also equal to F G, then F K would equal F H—making the nearer to the segment through the center equal to the more remote, which is impossible. <pause> Therefore, F A is greatest, F D is least, nearer segments exceed more remote ones, and only two equal segments fall from F on the circle, one on each side of the least. As was required to prove.
