Book Three, Proposition Twenty-Two. The opposite angles of quadrilaterals in circles are equal to two right angles. <pause> Let A B C D be a circle, and let A B C D be a quadrilateral inscribed in it. We are to prove that its opposite angles sum to two right angles.

[bell:construction]
[style: methodical, precise constructive cadence]
Join the diagonals A C and B D by Postulate One.

[bell:insight]
[style: luminous, deductive geometric clarity]
By One Thirty-Two, in any triangle the three interior angles sum to two right angles; so in triangle A B C, angles C A B, A B C, and B C A together equal two right angles. <pause> By Three Twenty-One, angle C A B equals angle B D C, for both lie in the circle segment B A D C; and angle A C B equals angle A D B, for both lie in the segment A D C B. Therefore the whole angle A D C equals the sum of angles B A C and A C B. <pause> Add angle A B C to each. Then angles A B C and A D C together equal angles A B C, B A C, and A C B—which equal two right angles. By the same reasoning, opposite angles B A D and D C B also sum to two right angles, as was required to prove.

[bell:system]
[style: reflective, scholarly, architectural synthesis]
Proposition Twenty-Two reveals how a circle constrains any inscribed four-sided polygon: its two diagonals partition one pair of opposite angles into four inscribed angles that reassemble inside a single triangle. As Heath and Todhunter emphasize, the converse is equally powerful: any quadrilateral whose opposite angles are supplementary is cyclic—a circle circumscribed about three of its vertices must pass through the fourth. <pause> In the GEOMETOR G-Index, node G one hundred twenty-five unites Book One's triangle angle-sum theorem, G fifty-nine, with Three Twenty-One's equal-segment angles, G one hundred twenty-four, across fifty-one ancestor nodes.
